## Interpretation limits

1. **Correlation is not causation.** A coefficient describes how two series move together; it does not show that one drives the other. Weather, emissions and pollutant levels share common drivers (season, time of day, monsoon regime).
2. **Confounding by season and daily cycle.** E.g. temperature and PM2.5 are both seasonal in Hanoi; a negative r mostly reflects winter vs. summer, not a direct effect of temperature. Compare within a season or use daily means before drawing conclusions.
3. **Modelled data.** Pollutants (CAMS) and weather (ECMWF reanalysis) come from models, and CAMS itself is driven by meteorological fields, so part of the pollutant-weather correlation is built into the model rather than observed.
4. **Shared sources among pollutants.** PM2.5, PM10, CO and NO2 are emitted by the same activities and dispersed by the same air mass; high r among them indicates common origin/transport, not that one pollutant produces another. PM2.5 is also a subset of PM10, and US AQI is computed from the pollutants.
5. **Pearson assumes linearity.** Pollutant distributions are right-skewed and relations such as wind speed or boundary-layer height vs. PM2.5 are non-linear; Spearman (rank) values are supplied alongside for that reason.
6. **Autocorrelation.** Hourly values are strongly serially correlated, so the effective sample size is far below n and p-values would be over-optimistic; they are therefore not reported.
7. **Unequal samples.** `carbon_dioxide` exists only from 2024-10-26 and `boundary_layer_height` is missing for 2024-01 to 2024-06; their coefficients use fewer hours (see column `n`) and a different period.
8. **Spatial scale.** One grid cell (~40 km for CAMS global, ~10-25 km for weather) represents each city; coefficients describe regional background, not street level.
9. **Pooled ('All cities') coefficients** mix between-city differences with within-city variation (Simpson's paradox risk); prefer the per-city values.
